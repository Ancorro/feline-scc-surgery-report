Surgery for Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma in Cats

What maxillectomy and mandibulectomy achieve, what they cost the cat, which cats are candidates, and what untreated cats face. Updated 21 October 2026.

This is a short report built from two retrospective studies, two PubMed abstracts and one trial flyer. It is not veterinary advice; every surgical decision depends on the individual cat's imaging, staging and anesthetic fitness.

Overview

Complete surgical removal is the only treatment in these sources under which most cats with oral squamous cell carcinoma (SCC, a cancer of the lining cells of the mouth) lived beyond a year. Cats with SCC treated by maxillectomy (removal of part of the upper jaw) had one-year and two-year survival rates of 83 percent, and the median survival time was not reached. [1] Cats treated by mandibulectomy (removal of part of the lower jaw) did worse, with a median survival time of 217 days for SCC, but still far better than untreated cats. [1, 3] Untreated or palliatively treated cats had median survival times of 44 to 45 days and a one-year survival of about 9.5 percent. [2, 4]

The price of surgery is a high rate of short-term complications. More than half of the maxillectomy cats had a post-operative complication, most often difficulty eating or wound breakdown, but almost all of these resolved within about two weeks and every cat returned to eating on its own. [1] After mandibulectomy, 72 percent of cats could not eat normally right after surgery and 12 percent never regained the ability to eat. [3]

Surgery is only an option for a minority of cats. The maxillectomy cohort had small tumours, with a median largest dimension of 2.0 cm for SCC, and no cat had metastasis to the lungs on pre-operative imaging. [1] The authors warn that the good results for SCC probably reflect selection of smaller, less diffuse tumours and may not apply to larger ones. [1]

What untreated cats face

Two independent cohorts agree that cats whose oral SCC is not removed live about six weeks after diagnosis. A New Zealand series of 52 cats that received at most palliative treatment had an estimated median survival time of 45 days, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 29.9 to 60.1 days. [2] A United Kingdom general-practice series of 54 cats had a median survival time of 44 days and a one-year survival of 9.5 percent. [4]

Where the tumour sits in the mouth changes the outlook for untreated cats. Cats with tumours of the oropharynx (the back of the throat) lived a median of 151 days, which was significantly longer than cats with maxillary tumours at 51 days, sublingual tumours (under the tongue) at 33 days, and mandibular tumours at 34 days. [2] Age, sex and the presence of bone in the biopsy sample did not predict survival. [2]

A tissue marker also predicted survival in untreated cats. Tumours with intense p16 protein immunostaining (a laboratory stain for the p16 cell-cycle protein) had a median survival time of 87 days, significantly longer than the 37 days for tumours with only peripheral p16 staining. [2] Staining for the p53 protein was not prognostic. [2] Both studies measured survival mostly as time to euthanasia, which owners decide for many reasons, so the figures carry extra variability. [2]

The United Kingdom study also found that cats given a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug after diagnosis had about half the hazard of death, with a hazard ratio of 0.46 and a confidence interval of 0.21 to 0.98, and the authors could not say whether this reflected pain relief, an anticancer effect, or both. [4]

Surgical outcomes and complications

The largest surgical series is a multi-centre retrospective study of 60 cats treated by maxillectomy at 14 referral hospitals between 1992 and 2016, of which 13 had SCC. [1] The comparison for the lower jaw is a 42-cat mandibulectomy series from eight institutions published in 2006, which the maxillectomy authors use as their benchmark. [1, 3]

Maxillectomy: survival and tumour control

Who was studiedSixty cats with a median age of 10.5 years had a maxillectomy for a biopsy-confirmed upper-jaw tumour, with at least three months of follow-up; 41 tumours were malignant and 13 were SCC. [1]
Tumour sizeThe median largest tumour dimension was 1.6 cm overall and 2.0 cm for SCC, with a range of 0.7 to 3.8 cm for SCC. [1]
Survival for SCCOne-year and two-year survival rates were both 83 percent, the median survival time was not reached, and survival times ranged from 23 to 2657 days. [1]
Local recurrenceRecurrence occurred in 18.3 percent of all cats, in 11.1 percent of cats with complete histologic excision and in 31.2 percent with incomplete excision; among SCC cats it was 15.4 percent. [1]
MetastasisOnly two cats, both with SCC, developed metastasis after surgery, to the regional lymph nodes in both and the lungs in one. [1]
Prognostic factorsLocal recurrence raised the hazard of death more than six-fold, adjuvant chemotherapy was associated with shorter survival, which the authors attribute to selection bias, and a higher mitotic index predicted shorter progression-free interval in malignant tumours. [1]

Maxillectomy: complications

During surgeryTen cats (16.7 percent) had an intra-operative complication, namely blood loss, low blood pressure or inadvertent compromise of the tumour capsule. [1]
After surgeryThirty-four cats (56.7 percent) had a post-operative complication, most often reduced appetite or difficulty eating (20 percent) and wound dehiscence or an oronasal fistula, meaning an opening between mouth and nose (20 percent). [1]
Other complicationsLip trauma from the opposing lower canine tooth occurred in 15 percent, nosebleed in 11.7 percent, and incisional swelling in 5 percent; one cat was euthanised 23 days after surgery because of flap necrosis. [1]
Eating and feeding tubesThe median duration of eating difficulty was 7 days, nine cats (15 percent) had an esophagostomy feeding tube for a median of 9 days, and all cats returned to voluntary eating. [1]
DurationOnly one cat had a complication lasting longer than 15 days, so the authors suggest a feeding tube need not be placed routinely at the time of maxillectomy. [1]

Mandibulectomy, for comparison

Mandibulectomy carries a heavier functional cost. In the 42-cat series, 72 percent of cats were dysphagic or inappetent immediately after surgery, 12 percent never regained the ability to eat, acute morbidity occurred in 98 percent and long-term morbidity in 76 percent of cats. [3] Even so, 83 percent of the 30 owners who gave information were satisfied with the outcome. [3] Cats with SCC had a median survival time of 217 days and one-year survival of 43 percent, and the local recurrence rate for SCC after mandibulectomy was 38.1 percent, compared with 7.7 percent as a cause of death in SCC cats after maxillectomy. [1, 3] Feeding tubes were used in 40.5 percent of mandibulectomy cats for a median of 74 days, against 15 percent of maxillectomy cats for a median of 9 days. [1]

Which cats are candidates, and what else exists

Surgery is offered to cats whose tumour can be removed with a margin of normal tissue and who have no evidence of spread. In the maxillectomy series, every cat had a biopsy or cytology diagnosis before surgery, 52 of 60 had chest imaging with no lung metastasis found, and lymph node imaging or sampling in 43 cats showed no spread before surgery. [1] Forty cats had a computed tomography (CT) scan of the head, which is what the surgeons used to plan the resection. [1] The tumours were small, with a median largest dimension of 2.0 cm for SCC and nothing over 3.8 cm. [1]

The authors conclude that maxillectomy is a viable option for cats with resectable, non-metastatic maxillary tumours, and they explicitly caution that the SCC results may reflect a selection bias for smaller and less diffuse tumours. [1] Complete excision matters: recurrence was 11.1 percent after complete excision and 31.2 percent after incomplete excision, and the authors recommend surgical revision or radiation rather than chemotherapy when margins are incomplete. [1]

Among untreated cats the most common sites were the maxillary gingiva (40 percent) and the sublingual region and mandible (23 percent each), and sublingual tumours had the shortest survival of any site. [2] Neither paper reports how many cats presenting with oral SCC turn out to be resectable, so no proportion is given here.

Practical points for an owner

Workup before surgeryThe surgical series relied on biopsy or cytology of the mass, blood tests, chest imaging and in most cats a head CT; lymph nodes were sampled in only a quarter of cats, which the authors list as a limitation. [1]
Where it is doneThe 60 maxillectomies were performed at 14 veterinary referral hospitals in North America and Europe, which indicates the procedure is a referral-level operation rather than general practice surgery. [1]
CostNone of the sources reports the cost of maxillectomy or mandibulectomy, so no figure is given here.
Feeding tubeAfter maxillectomy a tube was needed in 15 percent of cats for about nine days, whereas after mandibulectomy 40.5 percent needed one for a median of 74 days. [1]
Aftercare at homeOwners should expect reduced appetite for about a week, possible lip trauma from the lower canine tooth, and occasional nosebleeds, most of which were managed without further surgery. [1]

Non-surgical options named in the sources

For cats whose tumour cannot be removed, the maxillectomy authors summarise published median survival times of 60 to 174 days with palliative radiation, 86 days with an accelerated radiation protocol, 130 to 160 days with accelerated radiation plus carboplatin, 106 days with stereotactic radiation and 123 days with toceranib phosphate. [1] These figures are quoted from their review of other studies, which this report did not verify individually.

One investigational option appears in the handoff material. The University of Illinois announced a funded trial of intratumoral bio-adhesive nanoparticles carrying the chemotherapy drug exatecan for dogs and cats with soft-tissue-predominant oral SCC, given every three weeks for up to four treatments. [5]

Who is eligibleCats over one year old with a cytologic or histologic diagnosis of oral SCC that is predominantly soft tissue, preferably under the tongue, on the lip or on the gum line, in good condition with no other serious illness. [5]
Who is excludedCats with previous chemotherapy, radiation or immunotherapy, untreated comorbid conditions, or poor anesthetic fitness. [5]
What the trial coversThe trial pays for feeding tube placement, recheck bloodwork, the injections, biopsy and magnetic resonance imaging under anesthesia; owners pay for supportive medications and unrelated conditions. [5]
ContactThe flyer names the clinical trials coordinator, Rebecca Kamerer, at (217) 300-6453 or rmoss81@illinois.edu. [5]
Status caveatOn 21 October 2026 the flyer's original web address returned a not-found error and the trial was absent from both Illinois clinical-trials listings, so an owner should phone to confirm it is still enrolling before travelling. [5, 6]
